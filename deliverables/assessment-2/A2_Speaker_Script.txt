SLIDE 3 | 1:00–1:35 | Separate the outcome from its evidence
The intended outcome is improved access to suitable play resources. Toy redistribution is the activity, and items received are outputs. Family-reported access change is evidence about the outcome, rather than the outcome itself. Satisfaction describes service experience. Keeping these separate prevents a positive service rating or a toy count from standing in for improved access.

SLIDE 4 | 1:35–2:35 | Evidence informs indicator selection
Toy Libraries Australia's submission changes the proposed content. It reports wider choice and saving money as joining motives, describes uneven service access, and presents developmentally appropriate toys as a benefit. I therefore retain overall access change, cost barriers and perceived suitability. This sector submission reports market research without full survey methods. Lending also differs from redistribution, so it suggests candidates rather than validating this questionnaire. Availability remains a coverage gap because Q1 does not measure it separately. Q2 and Q3 provide context, while Q4 records use and Q5 asks about barriers. Transport, storage and household circumstances may help explain an answer.

SLIDE 5 | 2:35–3:45 | Measurement options and design choice
The matrix applies all four criteria. Relevance comes first because access change is the research question; feasibility then limits what fits before A3. Usable records can have low burden but mainly describe activity. Satisfaction is simple but weakly aligned with access change. Retrospective change needs one follow-up, but relies on recall. Prospective baseline and follow-up reduces reliance on recalled change, but needs two waves, comparable questions, linkage and enough time; attrition remains. My conditional choice is retrospective change plus contextual items if an eligible four-week follow-up fits. Prefer a suitable prospective design if feasible. If neither fits, audit existing questions and data. Burden and interpretation remain constraints. These are design judgements, not measured ratings.

SLIDE 6 | 3:45–4:15 | One main indicator; four supporting items
The items have distinct reporting roles. Q1 is the main reported access-change indicator. Q2 covers cost, Q3 perceived suitability, and Q4 recent use; these provide context. Q5 helps explain change and barriers. Report categories and valid item counts, while showing uncertain and skipped answers separately. Do not combine the answers into an access score.

Recall can distort the comparison, and responding families may differ from others. Specify the reference periods and say 'among responding families'. Without an adequate comparison, neither retrospective nor prospective responses establish programme effects. Records and interviews can help interpret accounts, but do not remove that limitation.
